Ollama Bootcamp · Lesson 06 · Running the model well

Does a bigger context make it dumber?

This is the one lesson with an honest "I don't know" in it. There is a well-documented effect where models lose track of things buried in the middle of a long prompt. Whether your model does it, at your context sizes, is a question only measurement answers — and the measuring is the lesson.

Part 1 ~6 minutes · Part 2 ~12 minutes, plus however long you let it run · 26 Oct 2026

Lesson 04 showed you can raise your context window by spending memory. This lesson asks the question that should come first: is a bigger window actually better?

Part 1

The idea

More context is not uniformly more understanding. Position matters.

The U-shape

In 2023 a Stanford-led team ran a careful experiment: give a model a long prompt containing one relevant fact, ask about that fact, and vary where in the prompt the fact sits. Nothing else changed — same prompt length, same question, same fact.

The result has held up well enough to become common knowledge: performance was highest when the fact was near the beginning or the end, and measurably worse when it sat in the middle. Plotted against position, accuracy makes a U. (Liu et al., 2023)

The keystone

A model does not attend to its context evenly. Material at the start and the end of a long prompt is used more reliably than material in the middle. "It's in the context window" is not the same as "it will be used".

Why this should change what you build

If the beginning and end are the privileged positions, then where you put things in a prompt is a design decision:

  • Your instruction belongs at the end, after the material it operates on — or at both ends. Burying "answer only in JSON" above 8,000 tokens of context puts it in the weakest position available.
  • A long conversation buries its own middle. In a chat, the oldest turns sit at the start (privileged) and the newest at the end (privileged), while everything from the middle of the session gets the worst position. That is a strange shape for a memory to have, and it is worth knowing it is the shape you have.
  • More retrieved documents is not strictly better. Adding a tenth document pushes the other nine toward the middle.

The honest caveat, up front

The U-shape was established on the models of 2023, mostly much larger and cloud-hosted. Whether qwen3.5:9b shows it in 2026, at the context sizes you actually use, is not something I can tell you from the literature. Architectures changed; long-context training got much better. The finding is a hypothesis to test on your own setup, not a fact to apply.

Do it: look for the effect

There is a tool in the repo for this. It hides one fact — a "secret ingredient" — in a wall of filler text, puts it at the start, middle or end, and asks the model to retrieve it.

  1. Run it at a modest size first:

    node assets/needle.ts --tokens 8000

  2. On this machine, that produced FOUND at all three positions — no U-shape visible at 8,000 tokens. Prefill took about 23 seconds per call. If you get the same, that is a real result, not a failed experiment: it tells you the effect is not biting at the size you work at.

  3. Push until something changes:

    node assets/needle.ts --tokens 20000

    Expect this to be slow — around a minute of prefill per call, so three minutes for the set. Go and make tea.

  4. Record what you find, including nulls. "No effect at 8k or 20k on qwen3.5:9b" is a genuinely useful sentence, and almost nobody writes it down.

If you remember only this
  1. Context is not attended to evenly — the documented weak spot is the middle.
  2. Put instructions last, after the material they apply to.
  3. Test it, don't assume it. At 8k tokens this model showed no effect at all.
Part 2

Designing the experiment properly

Including a flaw in the experiment this course already ran on you.

A confession about Lesson 02

Lesson 02 ran a context experiment: plant a codeword in the system prompt, send up to 48,050 tokens, see whether the model still recalls it. It did, every time, and the lesson concluded that the reported 4096 window was not a hard ceiling.

That conclusion stands — the prompts really were evaluated in full. But the recall half of that experiment was weak, and the U-shape is why: the codeword was in the system prompt, at the very start of the context — the single easiest position there is. Finding it there tells you almost nothing about whether the model could have found it in the middle.

This is worth more than the original finding. An experiment that only tests the best case will almost always succeed, and feel like evidence. The fix is cheap — vary the position — and it is the difference between "the model remembered" and "the model can remember anywhere".

What a careful version controls for

The original study varied position while holding length and content constant. (Liu et al., 2023) Borrowing that discipline:

ControlWhy it matters
Hold length fixed across positionsOtherwise you are measuring length and position at once and cannot separate them.
Use filler the answer cannot be guessed fromIf the model can infer the answer from context, you are testing its prior, not its retrieval.
Set temperature: 0You want the model's best attempt, not a sample from its distribution.
Repeat each cellOne run per position is an anecdote. Three or five gives you something to believe.
Score mechanicallyA regex on the expected answer, not your judgement of whether it "sort of got it".

assets/needle.ts does the first three and the last one. It does not repeat each cell — so a single run is suggestive, not conclusive. Extending it to repeat and report a hit rate is a small change and a good one.

Retrieval is the easy task

A needle test asks the model to find one fact. That is the gentlest possible long-context task. Harder, and closer to real use:

  • Two needles. Plant two facts at different depths and ask a question needing both. Failure rates climb fast.
  • Reasoning over the needle. Not "what is the secret ingredient" but "is the secret ingredient gluten-free?" — retrieval plus a judgement.
  • Negative controls. Ask about a fact that is not there. A model that confidently answers anyway is failing in a way a needle test never shows.

That last one is the most revealing and the most often skipped.

The cost side of the trade

Even if quality holds perfectly, context is not free — Lesson 02 and Lesson 04 measured both halves of the bill on this machine:

~50 tokens< 0.1 s7.7 GB
8,000 tokens~24 s—
20,450 tokens64 s—
48,050 tokens120 s11 GB

Two minutes before the first token. Whatever the quality answer turns out to be, the latency answer is already decisive for an interactive tool — which is a perfectly good reason to keep conversations short regardless.

Do it: run a real experiment

  1. Extend assets/needle.ts to repeat each position three times and report a hit rate rather than a single FOUND/LOST.

  2. Add a negative control: a run where the needle is absent, scoring a confident wrong answer as a failure.

  3. Run the grid at 8k and 20k. Two sizes, three positions, three repeats, plus controls. Budget half an hour of wall clock, most of it waiting.

  4. Write up what you found in three sentences — including, if that is what happened, that you found nothing. A null result you can defend is a better artifact than a positive one you cannot.

  5. If there is an effect at some size, you have found exercise 02. See Lesson 09 for turning it into one.

Check yourself

Read this next

Primary source: "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" (Liu et al., 2023). The paper that established the U-shape. Read the methodology section rather than the abstract — how they controlled position independently of length is the transferable part, and it is what makes your own version trustworthy.

Ask me things. This lesson has the most genuinely open questions in the course: "how many repeats before I believe a difference?", "is two needles a fair test or just a harder one?", "could the filler itself be confusing the model?" If your results disagree with mine, I want to know — that is a finding, not a mistake.

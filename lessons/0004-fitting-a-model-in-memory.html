Ollama Bootcamp · Lesson 04 · Running the model well

Fitting a model in your memory

Choosing a local model is mostly arithmetic, and the arithmetic is simpler than the jargon suggests. By the end of this you will be able to look at any model tag and say whether it will run well on your machine — before downloading 40 GB to find out.

Part 1 ~7 minutes · Part 2 ~10 minutes · measured on this machine, 26 Oct 2026

Lesson 02 ended with one rule: ollama ps should say 100% GPU. This lesson is how to make that true on purpose rather than by luck.

Part 1

The idea

Three numbers decide everything: how big the model is, how much memory you have, and how much of it the conversation needs.

Your memory is the budget

On an Apple Silicon Mac the CPU and GPU share one pool of memory. There is no separate graphics card with its own RAM — the 24 GB in this machine is the whole budget, and macOS needs several gigabytes of it to stay comfortable. Call it 18 GB usable and you will not go far wrong.

Three things have to fit inside that budget at once:

WhatHow big
The weights — the model itselfFixed. Whatever ollama list shows on disk, roughly.
The conversation — what it has read so farGrows as you talk. This is the KV cache from Lesson 02.
Everything else — your OS, browser, editorSeveral GB, and not optional.

Exceed the budget and the model does not fail — it spills onto the CPU and gets roughly ten times slower. That is the cliff, and it is the only outcome worth actively avoiding.

What the size of a model is made of

A model's size comes from two numbers multiplied together: how many parameters it has, and how many bits each one is stored in.

Parameters are what the tag tells you: 9b means roughly nine billion. Bits are what quantization sets. A parameter can be stored at full precision (16 bits) or squeezed down to 4 bits, which is lossy compression for neural networks — you lose a little accuracy and three quarters of the size.

Almost everything you pull from Ollama is quantized to about 4 bits by default, which gives a useful rule of thumb:

The keystone

Roughly 0.6–0.8 GB of memory per billion parameters at the usual 4-bit quantization, plus room for the conversation. A 9B model wants about 7 GB; a 70B model wants about 45, which is why it will not run here.

Check it against this machine: qwen3.5:9b is 9.7B parameters and 7.6 GB on disk — 0.78 GB per billion. The rule holds.

Choosing, in one table

Your RAMComfortable sizeReasoning
8 GB3–4B~3 GB of weights leaves room for the OS and a modest conversation.
16 GB7–9B~6 GB of weights. The sweet spot for most laptops.
24 GB (this machine)9–14B~7–11 GB of weights, with real headroom for context.
32 GB+24–32BMixture-of-experts models become worth a look here.

Keep the weights at or under roughly 60% of total RAM. The remaining 40% is not waste — it is the conversation and the operating system.

Do it: audit what you have

  1. See what the model actually is, rather than what the tag implies:

    ollama show qwen3.5:9b

    Look for parameters, context length and quantization.

  2. On this machine that prints 9.7B parameters and a context length of 262144. Compare that with what Lesson 02 measured in ollama ps: a context of 4096. The model can hold 256k tokens; Ollama gave it 4k. Capability and configuration are different things, and this is the clearest example you will see.

  3. Do the arithmetic for a model you are curious about. Parameters × 0.7 GB ≈ weights. Is that under 60% of your RAM? If not, do not pull it.

  4. Check your headroom while a model is loaded: ollama ps shows SIZE as the live footprint. Subtract it from your usable RAM — that remainder is what a long conversation has to fit into.

If you remember only this
  1. About 0.7 GB per billion parameters at the usual 4-bit quantization.
  2. Weights under ~60% of RAM. The rest belongs to the conversation and the OS.
  3. Too big does not fail, it crawls. A model that does not fit spills to CPU and runs ~10× slower.
Part 2

Reading quantization tags, and paying for context

What Q4_K_M actually means, how much quality 4 bits costs, and the memory the conversation quietly takes.

Decoding a quantization tag

Model files in the GGUF ecosystem carry tags like Q4_K_M. They decompose cleanly:

Q4Roughly 4 bits per weight. Q2 through Q8 exist; lower is smaller and worse.
_KA "k-quant" — a better method that spends more bits on the layers that matter. At equal file size, K beats the older style. (llama.cpp #2094)
_MSmall, Medium or Large within that family. Q4_K_M keeps some tensors at higher precision than Q4_K_S.

What does 4 bits cost you in quality? Using 16-bit as the baseline, Q4_K_M is reported at roughly +0.05 perplexity on a 7B model, with Q5_K_M at +0.014 for about 17% more size. (llama.cpp #2094) In practice the community consensus is that Q4_K_M is the default worth starting from, and that Q8_0 and F16 are "not recommended" for local use — much more RAM and markedly slower for a difference you are unlikely to notice. (llama.cpp #2094)

Your model is not a k-quant. ollama show qwen3.5:9b reports its quantization as nvfp4 — a 4-bit floating-point format, not a GGUF k-quant. The arithmetic above still holds (it is still ~4 bits per weight, still ~0.78 GB per billion), but do not expect every model you pull to carry a Q…_K_M tag. Read what ollama show tells you rather than assuming the naming scheme.

Rule of thumb

Given a choice between a bigger model quantized harder and a smaller model quantized lightly, at equal file size the bigger-and-harder option usually wins — down to about 4 bits. Below that, quality falls away quickly. This is folklore worth testing rather than trusting, and testing it would make an excellent exercise.

The conversation is not free

The KV cache — the model's memory of what it has read — lives in RAM alongside the weights and grows with the context. Lesson 02 measured it directly on this machine: SIZE climbed from 7.7 GB at a short prompt to 9.1 GB at 7,250 tokens to 11 GB at 48,050. That is 3.3 GB of conversation.

Which reframes the context-window question. Raising your window does not cost you anything on its own — it costs when you use it. The relevant arithmetic is:

weights  +  (context actually used × cost per token)  +  OS  ≤  total RAM

Ollama's defaults are set from this. Under 24 GiB of VRAM you get a 4k context; 24–48 GiB gets 32k; 48 GiB and over gets 256k. (Context length docs) Raise it with OLLAMA_CONTEXT_LENGTH=64000 ollama serve, and spend the memory deliberately.

Two knobs that buy memory back

KnobEffect
OLLAMA_FLASH_ATTENTION=1"Significantly reduce memory usage as the context size grows". Used automatically where supported; this forces it on. (FAQ)
OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPEQuantizes the conversation cache itself, default f16. "Significantly reduce memory usage when Flash Attention is enabled" — and it is global, affecting every model. (FAQ)

Both trade a little quality or compatibility for headroom. Reach for them when you are close to the cliff, not before — an unquantized cache on a model that already fits is nothing to fix.

Do it: find your ceiling

  1. Note your current SIZE from ollama ps with a short conversation loaded.

  2. Run a deliberately long prompt — node assets/needle.ts --tokens 8000 will do it — then check ollama ps again and record the new SIZE. The difference, divided by 8,000, is your approximate cost per token of context.

  3. Multiply that up. At what context length would weights plus cache reach your usable RAM? That number is your real ceiling, and it is almost certainly smaller than the 262,144 the model advertises.

  4. Optional, and genuinely informative: pull a model one size up (gemma3:12b or similar), run one prompt, and compare PROCESSOR, SIZE and decode rate against your 9B. You are looking for the point where it stops saying 100% GPU.

Reading the machine

A local model has two speeds, and almost every "why is this slow?" question is really asking about one of them. Part 1 is the idea, in plain words. Part 2 measures it on your own machine.

Part 1 ~6 minutes · Part 2 ~12 minutes, and it keeps · Ollama running, qwen3.5:9b · 26 Oct 2026

In Lesson 01 you saw that the schema guarantees shape and leaves truth to you. This lesson is the other half of running a model locally: nobody is managing the hardware for you, so how fast it goes is now your problem to understand.

Read Part 1 and stop if you like. It is the whole idea and one command. Part 2 is the measuring kit — useful when something is actually slow and you need to know why. It will still be here.

Part 1

The idea

No code, no numbers to memorise. One command at the end.

What happens when you press enter

You send a prompt. The model does two completely different jobs with it, in order.

First it reads everything you sent — your question, the system prompt, the whole conversation so far. It can look at all of that more or less at once, so this part is fast, and it happens before a single word appears on your screen.

Then it writes the answer, and here it has no choice but to go one piece at a time. Each new word depends on the words before it, so they cannot be produced in parallel. This is the part you watch streaming across the terminal.

An analogy that holds up well enough: reading a page is quick because your eyes can move around it freely; writing a page by hand is slow because you can only form one letter at a time. Same brain, very different speeds.

The keystone

A local model has two speeds, not one: the speed it reads your prompt, and the speed it writes the reply. They differ by roughly ten times. "Tokens per second" means nothing until you say which one you mean.

Why you should care

Because "it feels slow" is two completely different complaints with two different fixes, and you cannot tell them apart by feel alone — only by where the slowness sits.

What you noticeWhat is actually happeningWhere to look
A long pause, then text at a normal speedReading. Your prompt was long, or the model had to load from disk first.Shorten the conversation, or keep the model in memory.
Text appears immediately but crawls outWriting. This is the model's raw generation speed.A smaller model, or check it is on the GPU.
Everything is dramatically slowThe model did not fit in memory, so part of it is running on the CPU.The big one. ollama ps tells you in one line.

That third row is worth more than the other two combined. A model that does not fit in memory runs roughly an order of magnitude slower, and nothing else you tune will rescue it.

The five words you need

Local-model writing is thick with jargon, most of it naming something simple.

tokenA chunk of text, usually about ¾ of a word. Models count in these, not characters. "Gluten-free" is a few tokens.
prefillThe reading phase. Also called prompt eval — same thing, two names.
decodeThe writing phase. Also called eval or generation.
context windowThe most text the model can hold in mind at once, counted in tokens. Your whole conversation has to fit.
cold startThe wait while the model is loaded from disk into memory. Happens on the first request after a quiet spell, not on every one.
Skippable

You will also meet KV cache. It is the model's working memory of the text it has already read, so it does not have to re-read the unchanged start of a conversation every turn. It lives in RAM and grows as the conversation does — which is the mechanism behind a couple of things in Part 2.

Do it: one command

This is the entire Part 1 exercise. Add --verbose to the normal chat command and Ollama prints its own timings after every reply.

  1. Start a chat that reports its own speed:

    ollama run qwen3.5:9b --verbose

  2. Ask it anything — "explain why rice flour behaves differently from wheat flour" will do. Watch for the pause before the first word, then the speed of the words.

  3. Under the reply you will get a block of statistics. Two lines matter:

    prompt eval rate:    120.00 tokens/s     ← reading
eval rate:            53.00 tokens/s     ← writing

    They should differ a lot. That gap is the lesson. Everything in Part 2 is a consequence of it.

  4. Ask a second question in the same chat and compare. Then type /bye.

  5. In another terminal, while the model is still loaded, run ollama ps and find the PROCESSOR column. You want it to say 100% GPU. If it ever says anything with CPU in it, that is your answer to almost any speed question.

Check yourself

If you remember only this
  1. Reading and writing are different speeds. Say which one you mean before quoting a tokens-per-second number.
  2. A pause before the first word is not slowness — it is a long prompt, or a model loading from disk.
  3. ollama ps should say 100% GPU. Anything else is the problem, before you look at anything else.

That is Part 1. If you stop here you have the mental model and the one diagnostic that matters most — genuinely enough to work with. Part 2 is for the day something is slow and you want the actual numbers.

Part 2

Measuring it on your machine

The same ideas with instruments attached: exact timings, what they cost your own exercise, and one measured result that contradicts the obvious assumption. Come back to this whenever you like.

The receipt

Part 1's reading and writing have official names in the API, and this is where you meet them: reading is prompt_eval, writing is eval. Same two phases, now with a stopwatch on each.

Set "stream": false and every response carries six timing fields. All durations are in nanoseconds. (Ollama API reference)

FieldOfficial meaningWhat it tells you
load_duration"time spent in nanoseconds loading the model"Cold start. Non-zero means the model was not resident — a keep_alive problem, not a speed problem.
prompt_eval_count"number of tokens in the prompt"How much history you are re-sending on every single turn.
prompt_eval_duration"time spent in nanoseconds evaluating uncached prompt tokens"Prefill time. The word uncached is the interesting one — see below.
eval_count"number of tokens in the response"How long the answer was.
eval_duration"time in nanoseconds spent generating the response"Decode time. This is the number people mean by "tokens per second".
total_duration"time spent generating the response"Wall clock. Load + prefill + decode + overhead.

The docs give the formula outright: divide eval_count by eval_duration and multiply by 109. (Ollama API reference) That is the decode rate. The same arithmetic on the prompt_eval_* pair gives the prefill rate, and the two are nothing alike.

Your machine, measured

These are real numbers from this laptop — 24 GB Apple Silicon, qwen3.5:9b, mlx runner — not illustrative ones. Yours will drift a little run to run; the ratios are the lesson.

MeasurementResultReading
Cold load_duration3.41 sThe pause before your first answer is loading, not thinking. Default residency is 5 minutes. (FAQ)
Warm load_duration0.00 sStill resident. Nothing to do.
Prefill, fresh prompt~120 tok/sReading your prompt.
Prefill, identical prompt repeated~881 tok/s7× faster — the prefix was cached, so almost nothing needed evaluating.
Decode50–55 tok/sWriting the reply. Roughly a tenth of cached prefill speed.
Decode on the cold run27 tok/sDon't benchmark the first call. It is always a lie.

Why caching matters more than it looks. A chat resends the entire history every turn. Because the unchanged prefix is cached, the cost of turn n is dominated by what you appended, not by the whole transcript. That is what makes multi-turn chat affordable at all — and it is why a chat that edits its own earlier messages suddenly gets slow.

ollama ps, and the thing it does not tell you

One command, four columns worth reading:

$ ollama ps
NAME          ID              SIZE     PROCESSOR    CONTEXT    RUNNER    UNTIL
qwen3.5:9b    d63f830f28d5    7.7 GB   100% GPU     4096       mlx       4 minutes from now

PROCESSOR is the one that matters. 100% GPU is where you want to live. Anything like 48%/52% CPU/GPU means the model did not fit and part of it is running on the CPU, which is the single biggest performance cliff available to you. Ollama's own guidance: "avoid offloading the model to CPU. Verify the split under PROCESSOR". (Context length docs)

SIZE is not the file size. It is the live footprint: weights plus the KV cache for the context. Watch it grow as you feed longer prompts.

The deep end — skippable

What follows is the most advanced thing in this lesson: a measured result that contradicts what the documentation implies. It is here because it is true and because it shows what measuring is for, not because you need it to use Ollama. Skip to what this costs chef.ts if you would rather.

Now the surprise. CONTEXT reads 4096 here, which matches the documented default for machines under 24 GiB of VRAM. (Context length docs) The obvious conclusion is that a conversation longer than 4096 tokens gets truncated and the system prompt silently falls out the back. That is not what happens on this setup. Measured:

Prompt sentprompt_eval_countPrefill timeSIZE afterRecalled the system prompt?
Tiny49< 0.1 s7.7 GBYes
Oversized7,250~2 s9.1 GBYes
Very oversized20,45064 s—Yes
Absurd48,050120 s11 GBYes

Forty-eight thousand tokens through a window reported as 4096, with a codeword planted in the system prompt still recalled perfectly, and ollama ps still insisting on 4096 throughout. The runner grew the KV cache on demand; the reported number was the configured default, not a guillotine.

Hold this loosely. That result is from one runner (mlx) on one machine in October 2026. On llama.cpp-backed runners, exceeding num_ctx has historically meant the context slides and the oldest tokens — your system prompt — are dropped. The transferable lesson is not "overflow is safe", it is measure your own setup before believing either story, which is exactly what the receipt is for.

So the real cost of a long history here is not amnesia. It is two minutes of prefill and 3 GB of extra RAM. That is a latency and memory bill, and it arrives quietly.

What this costs chef.ts

Measured against the actual exercise, one retry round at a time:

Attemptprompt_eval_countReplyDecode
195 tok482 tok58.5 tok/s
2606 tok482 tok67.3 tok/s
31,120 tok482 tok70.1 tok/s

A recipe costs a steady ~482 tokens, and each retry round adds ~512 tokens to the prompt. chef.ts never clears history between wishes, so the cost compounds across a session: three wishes with retries puts you several thousand tokens deep, paying prefill on all of it, for context the model mostly does not need. The recipes stay correct. They just get slower to start and heavier in RAM.

The design question this raises: does chef.ts actually benefit from remembering wish #1 while answering wish #3? If not, the history could reset per wish — cheaper, faster, and arguably better recipes. If so, it should be trimmed deliberately rather than by accident. Either way it should be a decision, not a side effect of a while loop.

Do it: take your own measurements

Part 1 had you read Ollama's own numbers. This does it from code, so you can change one thing and watch what moves.

  1. Measure a cold start, then prove the cache exists:

    node assets/bench.ts --cold --repeat 2 "Explain why rice flour behaves differently from wheat flour."

    Run 1 shows a real load. Run 2's prefill should be several times faster on an identical prompt. Write down your two prefill rates.

  2. While it is still warm, check the split:

    ollama ps

    Confirm 100% GPU and note your CONTEXT and SIZE.

  3. Make it spill. Pull something that cannot fit — ollama pull llama3.3:70b is 43 GB against your 24 — run one prompt, and look at PROCESSOR and the decode rate. Skip this if you would rather not spend the disk; the number to expect is a decode rate roughly an order of magnitude worse. Knowing what the cliff looks like is worth one slow prompt.

  4. Now watch your own exercise. Run npm run 01, ask for three different recipes in one session, and after each one check ollama ps. Watch SIZE climb. That is your conversation history turning into resident memory.

  5. Decide the design question above, and write your answer into the exercise's Caveats or Stretch goals. An honest "this grows unboundedly and here is why we allow it" is a better teaching artifact than silence.
